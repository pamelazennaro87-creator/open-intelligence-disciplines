# Africa Electricity Systems – Comparative Risk Brief

**Focus:** Structural comparison of electricity systems and observable disruption patterns (physical vs cyber) across selected African countries  
**Anchor case:** South Africa  
**Orientation:** Defensive / analytical  
**Sources:** Utility statements, government updates, INTERPOL-related reporting, reputable open sources (2025–2026)

---

## 1. Purpose

This brief places the South African electricity risk surface in a wider African context. It highlights structural similarities and differences that matter for understanding where physical attack, criminal vandalism, system fragility and emerging cyber risk concentrate.

---

## 2. Comparative Snapshot

| Country | Dominant system features (public view) | Primary visible disruption driver | Cyber dimension in open sources | Notes |
|---------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|----------------------------------|-------|
| **South Africa** | Eskom (generation + transmission) + municipal distribution; recent recovery from chronic load shedding | Physical (cable theft, substation vandalism, armed attacks) | Research on DER/smart meters; limited confirmed large-scale cyber blackouts | Dual system: national stability ≠ local reliability |
| **Nigeria** | Fragmented (generation, TCN transmission, multiple DisCos); chronic capacity and reliability gaps; heavy reliance on private generators | Physical vandalism of transmission towers and lines (very high frequency) | Limited public confirmed destructive cyber cases on the grid itself | Extreme physical attack surface on high-voltage corridors |
| **Kenya** | KETRACO (transmission) + regional interconnectors (e.g. Ethiopia HVDC) | Technical faults and interconnector issues more prominent in recent public updates; physical risk exists | Digital monitoring systems present; cyber risk acknowledged regionally | Regional interconnection adds both resilience and dependency |
| **Uganda** | UETCL (transmission) central to national grid | Physical vandalism historically noted; cyber incident claimed (2025) | Qilin ransomware claim against UETCL (Aug 2025); INTERPOL African Cyberthreat Assessment referenced monitoring-system compromise | One of the clearer public cyber claims against an African transmission utility |
| **Egypt** | Relatively advanced national grid; high access rates; significant investment in upgrades and renewables | Less dominated by the same scale of reported transmission vandalism as Nigeria/SA metros in recent open sources | More advanced digitalisation / smart-grid trajectory described in open reporting | Often cited as a more mature African grid example |

---

## 3. South Africa vs Nigeria – Closest Parallel

### Structural similarity
Both systems show a **sharp separation between generation/transmission performance and the ability to deliver reliable power to end users**, combined with a **very high physical attack surface** on network assets.

### Key differences visible in public data

| Dimension | South Africa | Nigeria |
|-----------|--------------|---------|
| National generation trajectory (2025–2026) | Marked improvement; hundreds of consecutive days without national load shedding | Persistent capacity and reliability constraints; frequent grid disturbances historically |
| Main physical target type | Urban distribution (cables, substations, tunnels) – especially municipal networks | High-voltage transmission towers and lines (330 kV corridors repeatedly vandalised) |
| Frequency of reported transmission vandalism | Significant at distribution level; less dominant narrative at national HV transmission than Nigeria | Extremely high (TCN reporting dozens to 100+ vandalism incidents in a year in public statements) |
| Parallel “off-grid” economy | Present (generators, growing solar) but grid still central for most formal supply | Extremely large parallel generator economy; many users experience grid as intermittent by default |
| Institutional complexity | Eskom + municipalities | Generation companies + TCN + multiple DisCos |

**Analytical reading:** Nigeria currently presents one of the most intense public records of deliberate physical sabotage against high-voltage transmission infrastructure on the continent. South Africa’s most visible physical risk concentrates on urban distribution assets and municipal networks, while national generation has recently stabilised.

---

## 4. East Africa Notes (Kenya, Uganda, regional links)

- **Kenya / KETRACO:** Public updates emphasise interconnector reliability (e.g. Ethiopia HVDC) and technical faults alongside the need for system stability. Regional power trade creates both resilience and cross-border dependency.
- **Uganda / UETCL:** August 2025 ransomware claim by Qilin against the electricity transmission company, with INTERPOL’s African Cyberthreat Assessment placing the incident in a wider pattern of critical-infrastructure cyber risk. Physical security of transmission assets remains a parallel concern in public discussion.
- **Pattern:** East African cases show a clearer mix of physical risk and emerging cyber claims against transmission utilities than pure physical dominance.

---

## 5. Egypt – Contrasting Trajectory

Open sources frequently describe Egypt as having invested heavily in grid expansion, upgrades and renewable integration, with near-universal access and a more advanced digital monitoring posture than many sub-Saharan peers. It is often used as a reference point for what a more mature African national grid can look like. This does not eliminate risk, but the public disruption narrative is less dominated by the same intensity of transmission-tower vandalism seen in Nigeria or municipal substation attacks seen in South African metros.

---

## 6. Cross-Cutting African Patterns (Open Sources)

1. **Physical attack and theft remain the most consistently reported disruption mechanisms** across multiple countries (especially Nigeria and South African urban networks).
2. **Cyber risk is rising in visibility** (ransomware claims, INTERPOL assessments, smart-meter and digital-control concerns) but still appears secondary to physical sabotage in most confirmed outage reporting.
3. **Institutional fragmentation** (multiple utilities, weak municipal finances, parallel generator economies) amplifies impact when any single layer fails.
4. **Regional interconnection** (e.g. Ethiopia–Kenya) adds strategic value and new dependency surfaces.
5. **Generation recovery does not automatically equal distribution security** – a lesson clear in both South Africa and Nigeria.

---

## 7. Implications for Risk Monitoring

| If the monitoring focus is… | Highest-priority African signals (from public data) |
|-----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| Physical security of HV corridors | Nigeria transmission vandalism patterns |
| Urban distribution resilience | South African municipal networks (City Power and peers) |
| Emerging cyber on transmission utilities | Uganda UETCL-type claims; broader INTERPOL CI warnings |
| System maturity / digitalisation benchmark | Egypt trajectory |
| Regional dependency | East African interconnectors |

For a South Africa–centred watch board, the most useful external comparisons are:
- **Nigeria** – intensity of physical transmission sabotage
- **Uganda / regional INTERPOL reporting** – cyber claims against transmission entities
- **Kenya** – interconnector and regional trade dimension
